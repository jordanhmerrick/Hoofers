Yes, more detail comes. This pass added the dressing — flowers, tufts, rocks, a fence, a path, rooftops past the trees, a bag on his back. Two bigger passes come after it.

  1. Line it up

    Camera sits behind your golfer. Driver in hand, power meter filling, the flag waiting out there in the haze.

  2. Watch it fly

    The ball carries, the camera follows it, and the yardage runs up in gold. That number is the trophy moment.

  3. Now you walk

    He hoofs it down the fairway and stands at the ball with the cow, waiting. Then the card gives you your number: 92 feet, and 38 of them are done.

Where detail comes from

  • Set dressing — grass tufts, wildflowers, rocks, bushes, a fence, a walking path, tee markers, a sign, rooftops out past the trees. Added here, and it's the cheapest richness there is.
  • Modeling and texture — the next pass, and the big one. Real characters with faces and cloth instead of my spheres, wood grain on the fence, grass that looks like grass.
  • Post-processing — bloom on the sun, ambient occlusion in the creases, a depth blur on the far hills, a colour grade over everything. That layer alone is most of the Royal Match gloss.
  • Animation polish — weight in the swing, squash on the bounce, the cow's ears reacting. Motion is half of why those games feel expensive.

Still a mockup, and still a blockout. Every shape here is a sphere, box or cylinder I assembled by hand — no sculpted models, no textures, no post. That's exactly why it reads generic: generic shapes. The detail passes above are where a game stops looking like a placeholder, and they happen after the design is settled, not before. Nothing here is a decision.
